Project Writeup · Jul 6, 2026

Working Note: Our Solution, the Failures Behind It, and What the Data Taught Us

This note is the story of everything we tried: what worked, what failed, what each attempt was worth, and what the dataset itself had to say about it. The task: predict TVT (stratigraphic depth) for a horizontal well past its Prediction Start, given the well path, the gamma ray log, and a vertical typewell. Every validation number is a pooled, length weighted RMSE of dTVT in feet, from fivefold cross validation grouped by well, on 762 wells after dropping duplicates and colocated twins. We also lean on a second check we call LSO: leave whole spatial blocks of the field out of training at once, to simulate predicting in an area with no nearby wells. It ended up mattering as much as the main metric, and you will see why. One disclosure up front: the competition is still open, so for two of our changes we describe the motivation, the measured impact, and the lessons, but keep the exact construction private for now. Everything else, including every negative result, is shared in full.

1. Approaches we explored

We count seven genuinely different approaches over the campaign. Four made it into the final solution, in whole or in part. The other three died, and each one died usefully. For each: the idea, the numbers, and what we took away.

1.1 A structural surface plus an exact identity

Idea. If you know the elevation of the rock column at every map location, TVT past the anchor follows from the well path by simple bookkeeping. This sounded too clean to be true, so we verified it before building anything: plugging the true formation tops into that bookkeeping reproduces TVT to 0.008 ft RMSE over all training rows. It is exact. So one half of the problem is just what fig. 1 sketches: fit a smooth surface through the wells where the layer depth is known, then read it along the new well's path. All of that half's error comes from the surface.

Validation. A heavily smoothed interpolation over the roughly 770 training wells scores 11.5 (public LB 13.76). We then spent real effort trying to beat that surface with fancier machinery: Gaussian processes, coordinate networks, boosted trees on coordinates, local plane fits. Everything tied or lost, and the only knob that ever mattered was how much smoothing to apply. That was our first humbling.

Lesson. The surface is limited by how many wells you have, not by how clever you are with them, so we stopped trying to out model it. Its error grows with distance past the anchor and roughly doubles on isolated wells. Most of our later decisions trace back to those two facts.

1.2 Candidate generation plus selection (failed)

Idea. The gamma ray match against the typewell often has several plausible peaks, because repeated beds look alike and the log will happily match you to the wrong one. Our plan felt natural: generate candidate TVT values per row, then train a model to pick the right one along a coherent trajectory.

Validation. Generation went almost embarrassingly well: a union of complementary generators reached a candidate oracle of 0.85 ft, with the truth inside the top five candidates 99.87% of the time. Then selection refused to work, and kept refusing. Boosted rankers, sequence models, set transformers, marginalized decodes: every one collapsed back to the structural prior at about 11.5. What finally convinced us to stop was the harshest test we could design: a selector that was handed the true label still ranked the correct candidate first on only about 20% of rows, near chance among the aliases at the far toe.

Lesson. That number ended the argument. The failure is a property of the data, not of the models: nothing available at test time separates the right peak from its repeated bed twins row by row. We dropped discrete selection entirely, and it never came back in any form. It took weeks to earn that one negative conclusion, and we leaned on it for the rest of the campaign.

1.3 A continuous correction plus a combiner (the core of the solution)

Idea. The pivot that saved the campaign was to stop choosing and start regressing: predict a smooth continuous correction to the structural prior instead of picking among candidates. Fig. 2 sketches the gamma ray side: the typewell gives rock character versus depth, the lateral log gives rock character along the path, matching the two patterns ties the path to depth, and past the anchor a crowd of candidate trajectories is weighted by how well each one fits the log. On top of that sit two parts: a small learned matcher that turns the correlation between the lateral log and a reference curve into a correction, and a boosted tree combiner that sees several estimators at once (the matcher, classical particle filter and beam trackers, the surface) plus agreement and confidence features, and learns row by row whom to trust.

Validation. The matcher alone scores 11.04 against the surface's 11.57. Inside the combiner it produced our largest verified step, 9.33 to 8.245, and deployed, it took our public score from 13.76 to 7.090 in one submission. A gain that size made us suspicious, so we ran a leakage check we would recommend to anyone: hand each well alignment evidence computed for a different well and see whether the gain survives. It collapsed to nothing, which is exactly what you want to see. Later we rebuilt the reference curve the matcher compares against (the first withheld construction), and the combiner moved to 8.130 (public 7.128).

Lesson. Continuous regression beats selection on this data. And the reference curve matters as much as the matching machinery, a thought that pays off again in 1.7.

1.4 A whole well decode of the log amplitude (failed twice on the board)

Idea. Decode the gamma ray amplitude along the entire lateral at once with dynamic programming, instead of tracking locally, hoping a global view would resolve the aliases that beat us in 1.2.

Validation. This one hurt. It was the best single estimator we ever built on standard CV, 8.43 standalone, and it looked wonderful in the combiner. Then it failed on the public board. We retrained it under exactly the conditions it serves in, resubmitted, and it failed again: 7.588, then 7.480, against the 7.128 it was meant to beat. LSO finally explained what the board had been trying to tell us: in regions where the surface is good, the decode sharpens the answer; where the surface degrades, it cycle skips to 22 to 53 ft errors, worse than the surface itself.

Lesson. It was not adding information. It was amplifying the prior, and an amplifier looks brilliant exactly when you test it in friendly conditions. After this we adopted a house rule: any component whose quality depends on another component being locally lucky gets validated under the unluckiest realistic regime before it ships. That rule saved us at least twice afterwards, and we wish we had written it down two submissions earlier.

1.5 A neural network leg, and other model classes (partly failed)

Idea. By now a fair question was whether the boosted trees themselves were the bottleneck. So: try a neural network and a few other learner families on the identical feature matrix and let them fight it out.

Validation. On standard CV the network helped: blended with the tree model at the output level with a small, nested fitted weight, 8.130 to 8.023 (public 7.144). Under LSO it collapsed, with region errors of 12 to 22 where the trees held 9 to 11. The other model classes fooled us at first: they looked clearly better, and their entire edge turned out to ride one lucky feature, inherited from the 1.4 decoder. Remove it, and the trees won everywhere honest.

Lesson. Two things. An apparent model class gap can ride a single bad feature, so recheck the comparison after removing it; we nearly rebuilt our stack around a mirage. And a fragile leg is still usable if you combine at the output level with a bounded weight; sharing features between legs is what kills you.

1.6 Per well corrections: self calibration, residual models, routing (failed six ways)

Idea. Our worst wells are not noisy, they are wrong by a roughly constant offset along the whole lateral. Oracles quantify how much that would be worth fixing (fig. 3): a per well constant correction would take 8.13 to 5.33, a line to 4.64, a quadratic to 3.51. And the temptation is strong because this dataset seems to permit an honest attack: anchor the pipeline earlier, grade the model on ground it can be graded on, fit its bias there, and carry the correction forward.

Validation. The premise teased us. Bias over the first 1500 ft predicts bias beyond at r = 0.58, so the thing we wanted to fit is real and persistent. Then it dies exactly at the boundary we needed it to cross: transfer across the anchor is r = 0.03, and nested tuning chose zero correction strength on every fold. We did not take one no for an answer; five more attempts agreed from independent directions. A second stage model on our own residuals was worse than a shuffled control. Well level aggregates predicting residual trends had negative honest r squared. Residuals of neighboring wells carry no spatial structure to interpolate. Geological descriptors do not separate disaster wells from normal ones. A network reading the raw log sequences end to end recovered real dip signal, but its correlation with the residual was 0.076: it had spent all that capacity relearning what the model already knew. Related failures: routing each well between model variants reached AUC 0.60, and confidence gated fallbacks to the prior lost in every quality bin.

Lesson. The missing signal is real, the oracles prove that, but nothing measurable before drilling carries it. Our physical read is that the bias comes from rock ahead of and between wells, which no backward looking measurement contains. Hard wells can be flagged. Wrong wells cannot. Section 5 is this lesson turned into an uncertainty policy.

One harness bug worth confessing: our first self calibration test accidentally graded on the well's build section, where TVT legitimately moves hundreds of feet, and looked like a beautiful pass. A sanity check on simple strip statistics caught it before we believed it. Cheap sanity checks on intermediate quantities earned their keep all campaign.

1.7 Fixing the physics inside the trackers (the final wins)

Idea. By this point LSO had taught us which parts of the system deserve investment: the per well machinery, the trackers reading this well's own log, survives spatial extrapolation, while anything leaning on neighbors does not. And we realized we had never touched the trackers. They came from public notebooks (credited in section 7) and had been running essentially unmodified since the first week. So we went looking for their physical weak points, proposing each fix on physical grounds before measuring anything.

Validation. Fix one: give the trackers the rebuilt reference curve from 1.3. Every tracker improved standalone, and then the first integration made the total worse, by 0.13. The autopsy was worth the bruise: we had upgraded too many places at once, and two legs of the blend that used to disagree usefully started making the same mistakes together. Confining the change to a single leg, with the rest verified bit for bit untouched, passed every gate we preregistered: full chain 8.35 to 8.02, LSO 9.50 to 9.12, correlation between the legs down instead of up, public 7.067, our first improvement of public standing in eight submissions. Fix two: correct an assumption in how the trackers move between measurements (the second withheld construction). Chain 7.76, LSO 8.79, public 6.877, our biggest step since the plateau, and the moment the campaign finally felt like it was moving again. One discipline note: we had bundled it with a second plausible tracker change; measured knob by knob, the companion change hurt, so the fix shipped alone.

Lesson. Fix physics inside the component where that physics acts; the same insight offered to the system in a more passive place did nothing. Upgrade one leg at a time and watch the correlation between legs. Attribute bundled changes before shipping, because one of them is usually freeloading.

1.8 Shorter negative results

Kept brief because we deliberately spent less on them. Synthetic and augmented training data hurt the matcher outright (a stronger variant of it went from 10.94 to 11.11, and more augmentation made it worse still); the far toe problem is missing information, not underfitting, so extra manufactured rows have nothing to teach. Widening the matcher's search range did nothing, because the range was never the binding constraint; the matcher under corrects long before it reaches the edge. Calibration after the fact, shrinking predictions toward a risk estimate, and per bin recalibration all validated flat or worse.

2. What we learned about the data and the wells

  1. The target is small and mean reverting: median absolute drift 11 ft, ninetieth percentile 32. Holding the anchor value flat scores 15.92, while extrapolating the slope of the known section scores about 124. That gap sets the tone for the whole problem: any component that extrapolates a trend needs a leash.
  2. The gamma ray gives with one hand and takes with the other. Correlation with the typewell at the true depth is 0.755 per well (0.84 pooled), and the peak usually pins depth to under a foot. But one well in five has a second peak within 0.10 correlation more than ten feet away, and about a third of the post anchor gamma ray is missing, fortunately in tiny gaps. Logs carry per well gain and bias, so we normalized per well, never globally.
  3. The field is one smooth geological body: no cluster geology. TVD is not a substitute for TVT, because dip decouples the two within a well.
  4. Error concentrates hard, and the numbers deserve a moment (figs. 4 and 5). The far toe past 3000 ft holds 64% of all squared error. The worst 10 of 762 wells hold 29%, the worst 40 hold 47%, and 36 of those 40 are whole well bias failures, offset along the entire lateral. Separately, curvature measured on the known section predicts toe curvature with r squared zero, so the per well quadratic prize from the oracle ladder is not reachable by extrapolating anything the well has already shown.
  5. Isolated wells run about twice the error of densely surrounded ones, and cross validation grouped by well cannot see it, because a held out well keeps its trained neighbors. That one clause explains the most frustrating month of our campaign: seven consecutive submissions with better CV landed between 7.128 and 7.588 without beating the earlier 7.090, and all seven leaned on neighbor based improvements. LSO exists in this note because of that month. Across the whole campaign, CV gains transferred to the board exactly when the LSO number moved with them, and a chain CV gain converted to roughly a quarter of its size on the public subset.
  6. On applying different methods to different wells: we tried it explicitly and it failed (1.6). What survives is softer. The combiner receives per row evidence (tracker agreement, matcher confidence, distance past the anchor, local well density) and shifts trust continuously; one inherited leg of the blend picks its tracker mixture per well from simple observable properties like lateral length and geometry. In practice, wells with flat, sparse, or low contrast logs end up leaning on the structural family, and wells with clean logs lean on the trackers. That behavior emerged from the features on its own. Every time we tried to sharpen it into explicit hand rules, we made it worse, which by the end had stopped surprising us.


3. Physical meaningfulness, and where we drew the line

The backbone of the solution is the exact identity from 1.1: given the structure, TVT is bookkeeping, verified at 0.008 ft. The trackers are software versions of what a geosteering engineer does, follow the log and update a structural hypothesis, and both of our final improvements were corrections toward physical realism, proposed before measurement rather than found by search. The matcher exists because the physical signal (rock character versus stratigraphic depth) is real and strong, and its failure modes match the geology: repeated beds alias, quiet logs carry nothing.

There is also honest metric optimization in the system, and we would rather name it than dress it up. The blend weights and the output stack exist to reduce error, full stop. We kept that surface small: few weights, bounded, fitted on nested folds, and the main blend sits where our two validation axes intersect instead of at either axis's optimum, which costs standard CV score and buys robustness on unfamiliar ground. One more metric driven behavior we kept with our eyes open: the model shrinks large predicted drifts, which is optimal for RMSE but not physical. Removing that shrinkage helped the rare big drift wells and destroyed everything else, so it stayed, and we note it here as the compromise it is.

The line we held in practice: a change shipped only if physics proposed it or the data confirmed it on both validation axes, with one variable changed at a time against an identical baseline. Changes that improved only the standard axis were treated as fitting our validation scheme, not as gains, no matter how good they looked. Holding that line cost us weeks of tempting shortcuts, and it is also the reason our last two submissions moved the board.

4. What each piece contributed

Read top to bottom, this is the campaign in eight lines (fig. 6). Public LB in parentheses where we spent a submission.

  1. Hold the anchor value flat: 15.92.
  2. Structural surface plus the identity: 11.5 (13.76).
  3. Trackers and matcher as evidence in the combiner: 8.245 (7.090). The single biggest step.
  4. Rebuilt matcher reference: 8.130 (7.128).
  5. Neural leg stacked at the output level: 8.023 (7.144).
  6. The full deployed chain at that point measured 8.35 standard and 9.50 LSO. This number is not comparable to the combiner numbers above, because the chain includes blend legs kept for robustness rather than CV score.
  7. Rebuilt reference given to one tracker leg: chain 8.02, LSO 9.12 (7.067).
  8. Tracker motion assumption corrected: chain 7.76, LSO 8.79 (6.877).

Inside the combiner, group ablations say no single family carries the result: removing the confidence and agreement features costs 0.35, removing the tracker estimates costs 0.48, removing the structural group costs about 1.0.

5. Uncertainty: what the model knows about itself

The model has two native row level confidence signals: how much the tracker ensemble's plausible trajectories disagree, and how ambiguous the matcher finds the correlation at a given depth. Disagreement is the strong one. Realized RMSE rises monotonically across its quintiles, from 4.1 in the most confident fifth of rows to 11.9 in the least, about a factor of three. Ambiguity is weaker and mostly flags the extreme tail (fig. 7). Both are fed into the combiner as evidence, which is where they do their work: removing them costs 0.35 RMSE, one of the most damaging ablations we measured.

At the well and zone level the picture is easy to state, and easy to tell a geosteerer. Intervals near the anchor run at 2 to 5 ft, against 9 to 12 beyond 3000 ft. Densely surrounded wells are about twice as reliable as isolated ones. Wells with poor logs lean on the structural family and inherit its distance dependent uncertainty.

And then the honest boundary, learned the hard way in 1.6: these signals say where the model is likely to be less accurate, not where it is systematically wrong. The dominant failure mode is whole well bias, our confidence signals are uncorrelated with it, and every attempt to act on confidence after the fact (calibration, shrinkage, gating) made validation worse. So we use uncertainty in two ways only: internally as combiner evidence, and externally as relative reliability, interval by interval. We verified that boundary six independent ways before we were willing to write it down.

6. Closing

The gamma ray does pin stratigraphy to under a foot most of the time; the work was building a system that knows when to believe it, and proving each improvement was real rather than an artifact of a cross validation split that always gives a well its neighbors. Our best two moves were small physical corrections to the least glamorous component in the pipeline, found by asking where its assumptions disagreed with how rock behaves, and they were sitting in plain sight the whole time. The negative results took most of the campaign, and we would not trade them: they are the reason we knew where the remaining error lives, why it is not reachable from what a well has already shown, and what our confidence signals can and cannot promise. If you take one thing from this note, take the second validation axis. It is cheap and unglamorous, and it made the difference between the month our scores went nowhere and the week they moved.

7. Credit to public work

Parts of our system began as ideas shared publicly by other competitors, and we want to credit that plainly rather than present everything as our own.

  1. The tracker family and the overall stack shape came from public notebooks in this competition's Code section: a likelihood weighted particle filter with a per well noise scale and a beam search over the typewell grid. We worked from the version captured in this public rebuild notebook, one of several rebuilds of the same widely shared solution, with thanks to its author and to the authors of the earlier kernels it descends from. Our own work on that side was the validation protocol, the combiner evidence set, and the physical corrections of section 1.7.

If we missed anyone whose idea reached us indirectly, that is our oversight and not an attempt to claim it.
